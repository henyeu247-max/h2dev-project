For thousands of years of human existence, no one on Earth knew what they actually looked like. If you wanted to see your own face, your only option was a calm puddle of dark water, or a polished piece of black volcanic obsidian. And even then, what looked back at you was murky, distorted, and fleeting. In the 16th century, a single Venetian mirror was worth more than an aristocratic estate or a warship. The island of Murano in Venice held a brutal, state-enforced monopoly. Glassmakers were forbidden from leaving under penalty of death. If an artisan escaped, assassins were dispatched across Europe to slit their throat. The secret to true reflection was toxic. Artisans used an amalgam of tin and liquid mercury. Workers inhaled deadly mercury fumes day and night, suffering tremors, tooth loss, and madness. Most glassmakers died before reaching the age of 30. The luxury of seeing yourself clearly was literally paved with blood and poison. King Louis XIV of France could not bear to send French gold to Venice. His chief minister Colbert launched a covert espionage operation, bribing Venetian masters to defect to Paris. Venice retaliated with poison, but the secret was out. The result was the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles—a colossal propaganda statement that France had conquered light itself. In 1835, German chemist Justus von Liebig invented the modern silvering process using a thin chemical layer of real silver. Because folklore believed silver was a pure metal that rejected the unholy, the legend was born that vampires had no reflection in silver mirrors. When mirrors entered everyday homes, human psychology permanently changed. For the first time, people developed a third-person awareness of their own aging, self-criticism, and personal vanity. Today, you glance at a mirror dozens of times a day without thinking. But behind that smooth pane of glass lies 500 years of assassination, chemical madness, and imperial espionage.
